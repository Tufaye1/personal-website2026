How to Hire a Performance Marketer: What to Look For (and What to Run From)

The single most important thing when hiring a performance marketer is this: find someone who has personally managed real ad budgets and can walk you through the decisions they made with that money. Not someone who "oversaw" campaigns. Not someone who "collaborated with the paid team." Someone who logged into the ad platform, built the campaigns, set the bids, read the data, and made changes based on what they saw. Everything else, the certifications, the agency logos on their resume, the buzzwords, is decoration until you confirm that foundational skill.

I say this as someone who sits on both sides. I run campaigns daily at Eskimi, manage clients through my agency Truman, and I've hired (and passed on) plenty of marketers. The gap between a real operator and a resume-padded generalist is enormous, and it shows up fast once money is on the line.

Here is how to find the right person and avoid the expensive mistakes.

What Actually Makes a Good Performance Marketer

Before you write a job description, understand what you are really buying. A performance marketer is not a "digital marketer who runs ads." They are a business operator who happens to use advertising platforms as their primary tool.

The best ones share a few traits:

They think in systems, not campaigns. A good performance marketer does not just launch ads. They build acquisition systems. That means they think about the full funnel, from first touch to closed deal, and they understand how each piece connects. They know that a beautiful top-of-funnel campaign is worthless if the landing page leaks conversions or the tracking is broken.

They are obsessed with measurement. Not in a "I check dashboards" way. In a "I question whether the dashboards are even accurate" way. The best operators I have worked with spend as much time auditing their tracking setup as they do optimizing bids. Because they know that bad data leads to bad decisions, and bad decisions burn money.

They are comfortable being wrong. Performance marketing is a hypothesis-testing discipline. You launch, you measure, you learn, you adjust. The marketers who struggle are the ones who fall in love with their own ideas and cannot kill a campaign that is not working. The good ones have a clinical detachment from their creative. They will shut down their favorite ad in a heartbeat if the numbers say so.

They can explain the "why" behind results. Ask any candidate: "Your ROAS dropped 30% last month. Walk me through what happened and what you did." A strong marketer will give you a specific, structured answer. A weak one will say "the algorithm changed" or "seasonality" without any supporting evidence.

The Interview Questions That Actually Work

Forget "tell me about a successful campaign." Everyone has a polished success story. Here are the questions that separate operators from presenters:

1. "Show me an account you manage right now. Walk me through your structure."

This is the single best question you can ask. Watch how they navigate the platform. Do they click with confidence or hunt for tabs? Can they explain why campaigns are structured the way they are? Do they talk about naming conventions, budget allocation logic, audience segmentation? A real operator will light up. A faker will panic.

2. "Tell me about a campaign that failed and what you learned."

You are not looking for the failure itself. You are looking for the quality of the post-mortem. Did they identify the root cause? Did they change their process to prevent it from happening again? Or did they just shrug and move on?

3. "If I gave you $50k/month to spend on ads for [your product], what would you do in the first 30 days?"

This tests strategic thinking. A strong candidate will ask you questions first: What is your current CPA? What channels have you tested? What does your funnel look like? What is your sales cycle? A weak candidate will jump straight to "I would run Facebook and Google ads" without understanding the business context.

4. "How do you decide when to scale a campaign versus when to kill it?"

This reveals decision-making frameworks. Look for specifics: thresholds, time windows, statistical confidence. Beware of answers like "I go with my gut" or "it depends."

5. "What is your opinion on [current platform change]?"

Pick something recent. In 2026, that might be AI-driven campaign types, the deprecation of manual bidding options, or changes to conversion modeling. This tests whether they are actively learning and have opinions, or just running the same playbook from three years ago.

Portfolio Red Flags (Run From These)

After reviewing hundreds of marketer portfolios and profiles, here are the patterns that make me immediately skeptical:

"Managed $X million in ad spend." Without context, this means nothing. Did they manage it well? What were the results? A person can manage a million dollars in ad spend and lose half of it. The number alone is not impressive.

Only showing screenshots of dashboards with big numbers. Anyone can screenshot a good day. I want to see trend lines, month-over-month improvement, and context about what changed. A single day where ROAS hit 8x does not tell me anything about sustained performance.

No mention of testing methodology. If their portfolio is all "I launched this campaign and it performed great," ask where the testing was. What did they test? How many variations? What was the control? Performance marketing without testing is just gambling.

Certifications as the headline. Google Ads certified. Meta Blueprint certified. HubSpot certified. These prove you watched videos and passed a multiple-choice test. They do not prove you can manage a budget. Certifications are fine as a line on a resume. They are a red flag when they are the first thing someone leads with.

Buzzword density exceeding content density. If the portfolio talks about "leveraging synergies across the omnichannel landscape to drive holistic growth" but cannot show a specific campaign with specific numbers and specific decisions, keep scrolling.

In-House vs. Freelancer vs. Agency

This is not a one-size-fits-all decision. Here is when each model works best:

Hire In-House When:

  • You spend more than $50k/month on a primary channel
  • You need someone embedded in your product and sales process daily
  • Your business requires fast iteration on creative and landing pages
  • You have enough work to keep a specialist busy full-time
  • You want to build internal IP and institutional knowledge

Hire a Freelancer When:

  • You need deep expertise in one specific channel
  • Your budget is $10k-$50k/month
  • You want flexibility to scale up or down
  • You need a bridge hire while searching for a full-time person
  • You need an audit or strategic plan before committing to a long-term hire

Hire an Agency When:

  • You need coverage across multiple channels simultaneously
  • You want a team with built-in redundancy (nobody is a single point of failure)
  • You benefit from cross-client learnings and industry benchmarks
  • You need services beyond just media buying (creative, analytics, landing pages)
  • You do not have the infrastructure to manage marketing internally

The Hybrid Model

The smartest setups I have seen combine a strong in-house lead with specialized agency support. The in-house person owns the strategy, understands the business deeply, and manages vendor relationships. The agency handles execution and brings channel-specific expertise. This gives you the best of both worlds: institutional knowledge plus specialist skill.

What a Good First 90 Days Looks Like

If you hire a performance marketer and they do not do these things in their first three months, something is wrong:

Days 1-30: Audit and Foundation

  • Complete audit of existing ad accounts, tracking, and analytics
  • Map the full customer journey from ad click to conversion to revenue
  • Identify the biggest leaks in the funnel (where is money being wasted?)
  • Set up proper tracking if it does not exist or fix what is broken
  • Document current baselines: CPA, ROAS, conversion rates, cost per lead

Days 31-60: Quick Wins and Testing Framework

  • Fix the obvious problems found in the audit (wasted spend, broken tracking, poor structure)
  • Launch initial tests to validate or challenge existing assumptions
  • Build a reporting cadence that connects ad performance to business outcomes
  • Start building creative and landing page testing pipelines
  • Establish clear KPIs and targets with stakeholders

Days 61-90: Scale and Systemize

  • Show measurable improvement on key metrics
  • Present a scaling plan with budget projections and expected outcomes
  • Have a documented testing roadmap for the next quarter
  • Build dashboards that stakeholders can self-serve
  • Identify expansion opportunities (new channels, new audiences, new geographies)

If someone spends 90 days "still getting up to speed" without tangible output, they are not the right hire. Performance marketers should be able to find and fix waste quickly. That is literally the job.

Compensation Benchmarks for 2026

Salary ranges vary significantly by geography, industry, and budget under management. Here is a realistic breakdown for the US market:

Junior (0-2 years): $45k-$65k Knows one or two platforms. Can execute on a plan but needs strategic guidance. Good for companies that have a senior marketer already and need execution support.

Mid-Level (3-5 years): $70k-$100k Can independently manage campaigns across multiple platforms. Understands full-funnel strategy. Can build and present reporting. This is the sweet spot for most companies making their first marketing hire.

Senior (5-8 years): $100k-$140k Manages significant budgets ($100k+ monthly). Can develop strategy, lead a team, and connect marketing performance to business outcomes. Likely has experience across multiple industries.

Head/Director (8+ years): $130k-$180k+ Sets overall paid media strategy. Manages a team. Owns the P&L for acquisition. Works cross-functionally with product, sales, and finance. At this level, you are paying for judgment and leadership, not just execution.

Equity and bonuses matter too. At startups, equity compensation can add 20-40% to total comp. At larger companies, performance bonuses tied to ROAS or CPA targets are common and healthy. They align incentives.

For freelancers, expect $75-$200/hour depending on specialization and reputation. Monthly retainers typically range from $2k-$8k for a single channel.

For agencies, retainers start around $3k-$5k/month per channel, with many charging a percentage of ad spend (typically 10-20%) above certain thresholds.

The Hiring Checklist

Before you extend an offer, make sure you can check these boxes:

  • They showed you a live account and navigated it confidently
  • They explained a failure and what they learned from it
  • They asked about your business before proposing a strategy
  • They have opinions about current industry trends
  • Their portfolio shows process, not just results
  • They can explain their measurement approach beyond "I check ROAS"
  • Their compensation expectations match your budget and the market
  • They seem genuinely curious about your product and customers

Hiring the wrong performance marketer is expensive. Not just in salary, but in wasted ad spend, missed opportunities, and the time it takes to undo bad decisions. Take the time to find someone who actually knows what they are doing. Your budget will thank you.

What's the difference between a performance marketer and a digital marketer?

A digital marketer is a broad umbrella term covering everything from social media management to content creation to email marketing. It is a wide role that often focuses on brand awareness, engagement, and content.

A performance marketer is a specialist within digital marketing who is specifically accountable for measurable outcomes tied to business revenue. They manage paid advertising budgets, optimize for specific KPIs (CPA, ROAS, LTV), and make decisions based on data rather than intuition. Every dollar they touch has a target return.

The simplest test: if someone cannot tell you exactly how much revenue their work generated, they are probably a digital marketer, not a performance marketer. That is not a knock on digital marketers. It is just a different job with different accountability structures.

Should I hire in-house or use an agency?

The answer depends entirely on your spend level and growth stage. If you are spending under $30k/month on ads and need coverage across multiple channels, an agency or experienced freelancer gives you more expertise per dollar. They have built-in processes, cross-client learnings, and team depth.

If you are spending over $50k/month on a primary channel and need someone deeply embedded in your product, hire in-house. The institutional knowledge and speed of iteration you get from a full-time person is hard to replicate with external partners.

The best setup for growing companies is often a hybrid: a strong in-house lead who manages agency relationships and owns strategy, while agencies handle specialized execution. This gives you business context and specialist skill without the overhead of building a full team from day one.

How much should I pay a performance marketer?

In the US market in 2026, expect to pay $70k-$100k for a mid-level performance marketer with 3-5 years of experience. Senior roles with significant budget management experience command $120k-$160k or more, especially in competitive markets or industries with large ad budgets.

The most important variable is budget under management. Someone optimizing $500k/month in ad spend needs a different skill level than someone managing $10k/month, and compensation should reflect that. Also factor in the complexity of the role: multi-channel management, team leadership, and cross-functional work all justify higher pay.

For freelancers, rates range from $75-$200/hour. Agency retainers typically start at $3k-$5k/month per channel. When comparing these options, remember to factor in total cost: an in-house hire also costs benefits, tools, and management time. An agency includes those costs in their retainer.
